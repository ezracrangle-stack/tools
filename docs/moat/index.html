Measuring the moat

Two numbers from Michael Mauboussin and Dan Callahan's Measuring the Moat. Where a company's return on capital comes from, compared with its peers. And how much market shares move in its industry, which tells you how hard it is to hold on to an edge.

1. Where the ROIC comes from

Return on invested capital is NOPAT margin times invested capital turnover: how much of each sale is left after tax, times how many dollars of sales each dollar of capital brings in. Two companies can earn the same ROIC in opposite ways.

Mauboussin and Callahan's point: companies that win through differentiation, a product customers pay more for, tend to have high margins and satisfactory turnover. Companies that win through cost leadership tend to have satisfactory margins and high turnover. Comparing the two parts with the peers tells you which kind of advantage to go and look for, and so which questions to ask: what makes customers pay more, or what lets the company produce for less.

Use the same definitions for every company: the comparison only works if NOPAT and invested capital are measured the same way. Our ROIC calculator works both out from the filings.

2. How stable are market shares?

Stable market shares make it easier for a company to keep earning more than its cost of capital. Shares that jump around point to new entrants, changing technology, shifting demand or price wars. The measure, which Mauboussin takes from Bruce Greenwald, is the average of the absolute changes in each competitor's share between two years.

His rule of thumb: five-year changes of 2% or less mean a relatively stable industry; more than 2%, an unstable one. In the paper, US search engines and car makers moved about 1% from 2018 to 2023, web browsers and social media about 3%.

Concentration is shown too: the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) and the share of the top four. Mauboussin warns that concentration isn't reliably linked to value creation or stock returns, and that market share is the better link to profitability. It can rise because one company is better, or because of consolidation.

Which companies it works for

Any company with clear competitors and comparable accounts. The first part fits non-financial companies; for banks and insurers, invested capital doesn't mean the same thing. The second needs a market you can define, with shares from industry data, company filings or trade groups.

Where the method comes from

Michael J. Mauboussin and Dan Callahan, "Measuring the Moat: Assessing the Magnitude and Sustainability of Value Creation", Counterpoint Global Insights, October 2024. The paper ends with a checklist of the questions behind the numbers.
