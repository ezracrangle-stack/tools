How much growth is priced in?

A stock price pays for two things: today's earnings, if they just stay flat forever, and the chance to invest in ways that create value later. Michael Mauboussin and Dan Callahan call the second part PVGO, the present value of growth opportunities, and read its share of the price as a gauge of expectations.

Steady state plus growth

If a company's earnings stayed exactly where they are and it never made another investment that earned more than its cost of capital, it would be worth its earnings divided by that cost. That's the steady-state value. Whatever the market pays above it is PVGO: the value of future investments that create value. Growth by itself doesn't count; only growth that earns more than the cost of capital does.

The same idea works on a P/E. At an 8.75% cost of equity, a company with flat earnings is worth 11.4 times earnings. In June 2026 the S&P 500 traded at 21.9 times consensus earnings, so a big part of its price was paying for growth.

Two ways to measure it

Method A divides earnings by the cost of equity, as the paper does for the S&P 500 and for its company charts. Method B divides NOPAT by the cost of capital and subtracts net debt, as in the paper's example: NOPAT of $100 at 8% is $1,250 of steady-state value, minus $250 of net debt is $1,000; against $1,500 of market value, PVGO is $500, or 33% of the price.

If you don't have NOPAT, the helper takes operating income times one minus the tax rate, the shortcut the authors use in "Return on Invested Capital". The paper's own NOPAT also adds back intangible investment. For a company that spends heavily on R&D or customer acquisition, run the intangibles calculator first and use its adjusted operating income: for a growing company, that means a higher NOPAT and a lower PVGO.

What the paper found

For the S&P 500, PVGO averaged 35% of the price from 1961 to 2025. It was lowest around 1974 and 2011 and highest around 1999 and 2001. The lowest quarter of readings was followed by 11.6% a year over the next 10 years; the highest quarter by 7.6%.

For US companies worth $1 billion or more, from 1990 to 2024, the fifth with the lowest PVGO % had a median 5-year return of 8.7% a year; the highest fifth, 5.0%. The low half beat the high half by 2.6 points a year on average, in about 90% of years.

How to read it

A low PVGO % means the market expects little value creation, as long as the business isn't cyclical or in secular decline (peak earnings make the steady state look too big). A negative PVGO means the price is below the value of today's earnings held flat. A high one means the price depends on future investments that haven't been made yet.

The authors find it most useful at the extremes, and a poor tool for timing the market. For single companies the result is very sensitive to the inputs, which is why both methods show what one point of cost of capital does. To see what growth the price implies, use the reverse DCF.

Where the method comes from

Michael J. Mauboussin and Dan Callahan, "Opportunities and Expectations: The Present Value of Growth Opportunities in Valuation", Counterpoint Global Insights, June 18, 2026. The NOPAT helper: Mauboussin and Callahan, "Return on Invested Capital: How to Calculate ROIC and Handle Common Issues", Counterpoint Global Insights, 2022.
