Intangibles and earnings

Accounting books a new factory as an asset, but most spending on brands, customers, software and R&D as an expense. Companies that invest in intangibles look less profitable than they are. This calculator, from Michael Mauboussin and Dan Callahan's Intangibles and Earnings, treats that spending as an investment and shows what changes.

Why earnings mislead

A company that spends $1,000 on a machine that lasts 5 years books it as an asset and expenses $200 a year. A company that spends $1,000 to win a customer who stays 5 years expenses all $1,000 at once, inside SG&A. Same economics, different accounting. And the faster the second company grows, the more its reported earnings understate what it makes.

Mauboussin and Callahan's fix: treat the part of SG&A and R&D that is investment the way accounting treats capex. Take it out of expenses, put it on the balance sheet, and amortize it over its useful life. Free cash flow doesn't change. Earnings, book value and the multiples built on them do.

How much of SG&A is investment

It depends on the industry. The paper uses a 2022 study by Aneel Iqbal, Shivaram Rajgopal, Anup Srivastava and Rong Zhao that estimated, for 42 industries, how much of SG&A (without R&D) and of R&D is investment, and how long it lasts. It goes from 0% of SG&A in transportation to 80% in precious metals; the average is 54% of SG&A and 76% of R&D, with lives of 3.3 and 4.4 years. Many analysts capitalize the same share for every company, which hides those differences.

Why growth matters

If the spending doesn't grow, this year's investment and the amortization of past years end up equal, and earnings don't change. Growth opens the gap: this year's investment is bigger than the older, smaller years being amortized. In the paper's example, with 30% of $1,000 of SG&A as investment, a 3-year life and 10% growth, SG&A falls about 5.1%. If the spending shrinks, the adjustment lowers earnings.

In the paper's cases (2021), a pharmaceutical company's operating margin went from −0.9% to 14.6% and a business-supplies company's from 3.4% to 4.0%. For the S&P 500, net income would be about 12% higher.

Our assumptions

Spending has grown at a steady rate, and each year's investment is amortized in a straight line from the year after. The paper adds the change to net income before tax; leave the tax rate blank to do the same, or fill it in to move net income and book value after tax. The reading's cut-offs are ours.

Which companies it works for

Any company that invests through SG&A or R&D: software, consumer brands, pharma, services. It moves little for companies whose investment is mostly capex. Not for banks and insurers. Pair it with the ROIC calculator: capitalizing intangibles raises invested capital too, which usually brings very high ROICs down to earth.

Where the method comes from

Michael J. Mauboussin and Dan Callahan, "Intangibles and Earnings: Improving the Usefulness of Financial Statements", Counterpoint Global Insights, April 12, 2022. Industry figures: Aneel Iqbal, Shivaram Rajgopal, Anup Srivastava and Rong Zhao, "Value of Internally Generated Intangible Capital", working paper, February 2022.
