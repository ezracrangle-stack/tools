Buybacks and stock comp

Lots of companies pay their people in stock and buy stock back at the same time. If the stock is cheap, issuing it hurts the holders. If it's expensive, buying it back hurts them. Michael Mauboussin and Dan Callahan's question: which one is it?

Which one is it?

Paying people in stock is issuing stock. A buyback is retiring it. A company that does both a lot at once is selling and buying its own shares, and only one of those can be at a good price. If the stock is worth more than its price, the buybacks help the holders who stay and the stock pay costs them. If it's worth less, the reverse.

Many companies size their buybacks to keep the share count flat. That sounds prudent, but it decides the buyback by the stock pay, not by the price.

The paper's numbers

Mauboussin and Callahan took Russell 3000 companies with sales over $1 billion and averaged, for 2021 to 2023, stock-based compensation over sales and gross buybacks over market value. The medians were 0.8% and 1.5%. Splitting the companies at those medians gave four groups. Their average annual returns: 13.7% for low SBC and high buybacks, 9.4% for low and low, 5.3% for high and high, and −0.1% for high SBC with few buybacks. The Russell 3000 returned 8.5% a year.

They're clear that many other things drove those returns. Over 5 years the best and worst groups stayed the same, but the two middle groups swapped places.

Dilution

Dilution is 1 minus the shares before over the shares after. A company with 1,000 shares that issues 100 more dilutes its holders by 9.1%: they now own 90.9% of it. Smaller companies dilute more; in the paper, the largest companies' median dilution over 3 years was negative, because buybacks more than offset the stock they issued.

Who wins from a buyback

A buyback doesn't create or destroy value for the company. It moves value between shareholders. In the paper's example, a company worth $100,000 with 1,000 shares spends $20,000 on a buyback. At $200 a share, double its value, the holders who stay lose $11.11 a share and the sellers gain $100. At $50, half its value, the holders who stay gain $33.33 a share.

That's why the value estimate matters more than the buyback itself. Use the reverse DCF or the earnings power value calculator to estimate it.

Which companies it works for

Any listed company that pays in stock or buys back stock. Stock pay matters most in technology and healthcare, where it's highest as a share of sales. The paper's medians come from US companies with sales over $1 billion, so use them as a reference, not a rule, for smaller ones.

Where the method comes from

Michael J. Mauboussin and Dan Callahan, "Which One Is It? Equity Issuance and Retirement", Counterpoint Global Insights, July 2024. The buyback example: Mauboussin and Callahan, "Capital Allocation: Results, Analysis, and Assessment", Counterpoint Global Insights, December 2022, Exhibit 39, from Mauboussin and Rappaport's Expectations Investing.
