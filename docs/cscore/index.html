Montier's C-Score

C for cooking the books, or cheating. James Montier's score from Value Investing counts how many of six common earnings manipulations a company shows, from earnings that pull away from cash flow to receivables and inventory growing faster than sales.

You need two years of the income statement, cash flow statement and balance sheet. Nothing you type leaves your browser.

How it's calculated

Each of the six flags scores 1 when it's up, and the C-Score is the total. Montier built it after noticing that short sellers are the most careful readers of accounts. In his words, they're closer to being the accounting police than rumour mongers.

  • Earnings pulling away from cash flow. Earnings rest on estimates (bad debts, pension returns, capitalized costs); management has less room to shape cash.
  • Days sales outstanding rising. Receivables growing faster than sales: the sign of stuffing the channel.
  • Days of inventory rising. Inventory piling up usually means sales are slowing.
  • Other current assets rising against sales. Investors watch receivables and inventory, so a careful CFO may use the catch-all line instead.
  • Depreciation falling against gross PP&E. Stretching the assumed life of assets is an easy way to beat the quarter.
  • High asset growth. Serial acquirers can use their deals to blur their earnings.

How to read the result

In Montier's test (1993 to 2007), stocks with high C-Scores trailed the market by about 8% a year in the USA and 5% in Europe. It works best with valuation: expensive stocks are more tempted to keep up appearances, and punished harder when they're found out. A C-Score of 5 with a price to sales above 2 lost about 4% a year in both regions, and more than half of those stocks fell.

Montier calls the score only a first step. Read the notes behind every flag that's up: a jump in inventory before a product launch, or asset growth from one sensible acquisition, can have an innocent explanation.

Which companies it works for

Industrial, retail, consumer and technology companies with receivables, inventory and fixed assets. For banks and insurers most of the lines don't apply, and for companies with no inventory the third flag never goes up.

Where the method comes from

James Montier, Value Investing: Tools and Techniques for Intelligent Investment (Wiley, 2009), chapter 25, "Cooking the Books, or, More Sailing Under the Black Flag". The threshold for high asset growth is ours: the book doesn't give one.
