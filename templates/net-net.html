Net-net calculator

A net-net is a stock that sells for less than its current assets minus all of its liabilities. Buy one and you get the factories and the brand for free. (In theory. Keep reading.)

Ben Graham called that number net current asset value, or NCAV, and liked to pay two-thirds of it or less. Copy the balance sheet in, top to bottom, and the worksheet does the arithmetic. Nothing you type leaves your browser.

What this calculator can't tell you

The worksheet gives you a number. The 10-K tells you if the number is real. Four things I check before I trust it:

Whether the inventory sells

NCAV counts inventory at 100% and net net working capital at 50%. Last season's clothes or parts for a discontinued machine can be worth much less. Read the inventory note and check whether days of inventory keep climbing.

What isn't on the balance sheet

Unfunded pensions, retiree health costs, lawsuits, guarantees and purchase commitments show up in the notes, if they show up at all. US companies have carried most leases on the balance sheet since 2019; the rest still hides in the footnotes. Add what you find to the off-balance-sheet line and watch modern NCAV.

How fast the cash goes

A company with $30 million of NCAV that loses $10 million a year has three years before the discount is gone. Run last year's balance sheet through the calculator too. If NCAV fell more than 25% in 12 months, I'd want to know why before buying.

Whether anything closes the gap

Some net-nets stay net-nets for a decade. Look for a reason the price could catch up: a buyback, a sale of the company, an activist, insiders buying with their own money. A CEO who keeps getting paid well while the stock sits at its lows, and no shareholder big enough to replace him, usually means a value trap.

Where the method comes from

Benjamin Graham laid out the net current asset test with David Dodd in Security Analysis (1934) and came back to it in The Intelligent Investor. His rule was to pay no more than two-thirds of NCAV and to own a lot of them, so a few disasters wouldn't sink the portfolio.

Walter Schloss worked for Graham at Graham-Newman and ran his own partnership from 1955 to 2002, buying cheap stocks against their book value and staying away from debt. Buffett put him in "The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville" in 1984. The Schloss line in the worksheet takes long-term debt out of working capital for that reason.

The modern version of NCAV also subtracts off-balance-sheet liabilities and preferred stock, because both get paid before common shareholders.
