A. Montier's C-Score

C for cooking the books. Each of six flags scores 1 when it's up, and the C-Score is the total. Montier built it after noticing that short sellers are the most careful readers of accounts.

In his test (1993 to 2007), stocks with high C-Scores trailed the market by about 8% a year in the USA and 5% in Europe. It works best with valuation: a C-Score of 5 with a price to sales above 2 lost about 4% a year in both regions, and more than half of those stocks fell. The threshold for high asset growth is ours: the book doesn't give one.

B. Beneish's M-Score

Messod Beneish compared 74 companies caught manipulating their earnings with thousands that weren't, and found eight ratios that told them apart. The M-Score weighs them in one number. Above −1.78, a company looks like the manipulators in his sample. Many analysts use −2.22 as a softer cut, so we show the space between the two as a grey zone.

The ratio with the most weight is total accruals: earnings that don't show up as cash. Growth and leverage are in the model as motives, not as signs of manipulation.

C. Schilit's checks

Four quick tests from Financial Shenanigans, each aimed at a trick the book documents:

  • Free cash flow after acquisitions (chapter 16). Buying companies can lift cash flow from operations, because the cash spent shows up as investing. Serial acquirers can report strong operating cash flow while burning cash overall.
  • Days payable outstanding (chapter 12). Paying suppliers later lifts operating cash flow, but only once: it can't go on forever.
  • Allowance for doubtful accounts (chapter 7). The allowance usually grows with receivables. When it shrinks as receivables rise, the company may be booking too little bad-debt expense.
  • Deferred revenue (chapter 8). It's cash collected for work not yet done. When it falls while sales grow, the company may be pulling future revenue into today.

How to read the result

All three are screens. A jump in inventory before a product launch, slower payments negotiated with suppliers, or one sensible acquisition can each raise a flag for innocent reasons. Read the notes behind every flag that's up. When the three checks point the same way, look harder.

Which companies it works for

Industrial, retail, consumer and technology companies with receivables, inventory and fixed assets. Not banks or insurers: Beneish left them out of his study, and most of the lines don't apply.
