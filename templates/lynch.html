Peter Lynch's PEG

"The p/e ratio of any company that's fairly priced will equal its growth rate." Peter Lynch's rule of thumb from One Up on Wall Street, with the dividend-adjusted version he gives next to it and his trick of taking the cash out of the price, as he did with Ford.

How it's calculated

The PEG divides the P/E by the growth rate of earnings, taken as a whole number: a P/E of 15 on 15% growth is 1. Lynch: a company with 12% growth and a P/E of 6 is very attractive; one with 6% growth and a P/E of 12 is headed for a comedown.

The dividend-adjusted version adds the dividend yield to the growth and divides by the P/E. His example: 12% growth plus a 3% yield, over a P/E of 10, gives 1.5. Below 1 is poor, 1.5 is okay, and 2 or better is what you're looking for.

Cash changes the picture. In 1988 Ford traded at $38 with $16.30 a share in cash beyond its debt, so Lynch was really paying $21.70 for the car business: a P/E of 3.1, not 5.4. The calculator does the same with the net cash you give it.

Section 3 is Lynch's argument for fast growers. At the same PEG, a 20% grower at 20 times earnings beats a 10% grower at 10 times: after ten years its earnings are $6.19 against $2.60, and even at a P/E of 15 the stock is worth $92.85 against $26.

Which companies it works for

Lynch sorts companies into six categories. The PEG is built for two of them: stalwarts, which grow 10% to 12% a year, and fast growers, at 20% to 25%. For slow growers the dividend-adjusted ratio is the better guide.

It works badly for the other three:

  • Cyclicals. Their P/E is lowest at the top of the cycle, when earnings are about to fall. A low PEG there is a trap.
  • Turnarounds. Earnings are low or negative, so the P/E says little.
  • Asset plays. The value is in what the company owns, not in what it earns. Look at the net cash and the assets instead.

What it can't tell you

The growth rate

It's the number everything rests on, and it's the future one that counts. Lynch's advice: the growth that matters is of earnings, his favorites grow 20% to 25% a year, he's wary of anything faster than 25%, and suspicious of 50% to 100%. That pace is very hard to keep up for three years, let alone ten.

Whether the earnings are real

A PEG on inflated earnings is a precise answer to the wrong question. Check the cash flow and the balance sheet too.

Where the method comes from

From One Up on Wall Street by Peter Lynch with John Rothchild, chapter 13, "Some Famous Numbers": the sections on the price/earnings ratio, the cash position and the growth rate.
