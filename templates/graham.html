Graham's formulas

Benjamin Graham left two rules of thumb that get quoted everywhere. One prices a stock from its earnings and growth. The other, now called the Graham number, caps what a careful investor should pay. Both come from The Intelligent Investor, and this page uses them the way he wrote them.

Which companies it works for

Large, steady, profitable companies with a long record, which is the universe Graham had in mind for the defensive investor. He also wanted them held in a portfolio of 10 to 30 stocks, never one at a time.

It works badly for four kinds of company:

  • Fast growers. Above about 8% a year, Graham himself said no formula values a company realistically.
  • Companies that lose money, or earn at a cyclical peak. The formulas multiply earnings, so use normal earnings or don't use them.
  • Banks, insurers and utilities. The current-ratio test doesn't fit them. Graham gave utilities a different debt rule.
  • Businesses built on brands or software. Their book value leaves out most of what they own, so they fail the price-to-book (P/B) test almost by design.

What this calculator can't tell you

The growth rate

In the formula, the 7-to-10-year growth rate decides almost everything. Graham printed it with a warning: such projections aren't reliable, and prices won't follow them. The number to look at is the growth the price implies. Then ask if the business can deliver it.

Normal earnings

Graham asked for "current (normal)" earnings. One good year at the top of a cycle can make any stock look cheap.

The bond yield

The 1974 version leans on AAA bond yields, and few companies are rated AAA today. Use the highest-grade corporate yield you can find in the company's currency, and say which one you used.

The business

Graham's tests check size, balance sheet, record and price. They say nothing about competition, management or where the industry is going.

Where the method comes from

The growth formula is in chapter 11 of The Intelligent Investor (revised edition, 1973). The bond-yield version comes from Graham's 1974 article "The Decade 1965-1974: Its Significance for Financial Analysts." The book never uses the name Graham number: it's the cap on P/E times price-to-book (P/B) in chapter 14, which also lists the other five tests.
