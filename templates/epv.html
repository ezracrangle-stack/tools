Earnings power value calculator

What is a business worth if its cash profits never grow? That's its earnings power value, or EPV. Put it next to the share price and you see how much of what you pay is for the business as it is today, and how much is a bet on growth.

The worksheet follows Phil Oakley's method from How to Pick Quality Shares: EPV, owner earnings, cash yield and a maximum price. Copy the numbers from the annual report, top to bottom. Nothing you type leaves your browser.

Which companies it works for

Profitable businesses with a few years of steady results: consumer brands, distributors, services, small industrial firms that have been around a while. Their profits and capex this year say something about next year, and that's what an EPV needs.

It works badly for four kinds of company:

  • Banks and insurers. Debt is their raw material and they don't report EBIT or capex the same way. Value them on book value and return on equity.
  • Companies that lose money. With no cash profits there's no earnings power to value. The balance sheet tells you more; try the net-net calculator.
  • Cyclicals at a peak or a trough. Miners, steelmakers, airlines, homebuilders. One year's profits mislead, so average a full cycle before you trust the EPV.
  • Young, fast-growing companies. The EPV will be a small slice of the price. That's the method doing its job, telling you how much of the price rides on growth, but it can't tell you if that growth will come.

What this calculator can't tell you

The worksheet takes your numbers at face value. Four of them decide almost everything, and none comes straight from the annual report:

Maintenance capex

It moves the EPV more than any other input, and few companies report it. Oakley's fix is the 5- or 10-year average capex, which also counts some growth spending, so it errs on the safe side. If capex keeps running well above D&A year after year, find out why before you trust the result.

Whether today's profits are normal

EPV assumes this year's cash profits last forever. A cyclical business at the top of its cycle will look cheap, and at the bottom it will look expensive. Look at 10 years of margins before you pick the EBIT.

Whether the growth will pay

The worksheet tells you how much of the price is growth. It can't tell you if the growth will come, or if it will earn more than it costs. A company that reinvests at returns below your required rate grows and loses value at the same time.

The rate

Raise the required return from 9% to 10% and the EPV drops 10%. Use Oakley's ranges, then try one point higher before you decide.

Where the method comes from

Phil Oakley spent ten years as an analyst in the City of London and wrote How to Pick Quality Shares (Harriman House, 2016) for private investors. His EPV is a simpler version of the earnings power value Bruce Greenwald teaches at Columbia, which goes back to Graham and Dodd.

Owner earnings come from Warren Buffett's 1986 letter to Berkshire shareholders. So does the idea behind the maximum price: divide owner earnings by the government bond yield. Oakley swaps the bond yield for inflation plus 3%, because with bond yields near zero, Buffett's version justifies almost any price.
